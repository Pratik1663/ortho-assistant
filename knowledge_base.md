## SECTION 0 — BIOMECHANICAL FOUNDATIONS (the "why" behind every mod)

These principles get referenced throughout. The AI should reason from them when a presentation doesn't match a canned entry.

**0.1 Load redistribution, not load removal.** An orthosis can't delete body weight. Every offloading mod works by moving load somewhere else: an aperture shifts pressure to the surrounding rim, a met pad shifts it proximally to the met shafts, a horseshoe pad shifts it to the heel periphery. Always ask: where is the load going, and can that tissue take it?

**0.2 Pressure = force / area.** Focal pain usually means high pressure over a small area (prominent met head, spur point, fibroma). Two fixes: spread the same force over more area (contour, cushioning, total-contact fit) or remove contact at the point (aperture, cutout, channel). Focal problem → focal solution. Diffuse problem → distributed solution.

**0.3 The three rockers.** Heel rocker (loading response, calcaneus pivots), ankle rocker (midstance, tibia advances over the foot), forefoot rocker (propulsion, pivot over the met heads with 1st MTP dorsiflexion). Mods target rockers: heel cushions and lifts work at the heel rocker, shell rigidity and arch contour at the ankle rocker, Morton's/reverse Morton's/kinetic wedge at the forefoot rocker.

**0.4 The windlass mechanism.** Hallux dorsiflexion in propulsion winds the plantar fascia around the 1st met head, raising the arch and locking the midfoot into a rigid lever. For the windlass to engage, the 1st ray must be able to plantarflex. If the 1st ray is held up (by ground, shoe, or the orthotic shell itself), 1st MTP dorsiflexion is blocked → functional hallux limitus → compensation up the chain (late midstance pronation, low-gear push-off through the lesser mets, hip/low back strain). This single mechanism explains the entire "free the first ray" family: 1st Ray Cut Out, Reverse Morton's, Kinetic Wedge.

**0.5 STJ axis and moment arms (Kirby's rotational equilibrium).** The subtalar joint axis position determines whether ground reaction force pronates or supinates the foot. A medially deviated axis means most of the plantar surface lies lateral to the axis → large pronation moments → the foot needs supination moments from the device (medial skive, medial flange, varus posting, firm shell). A laterally deviated axis (cavus, sprain-prone feet) → the opposite (lateral skive/posting, lateral flange). Flanges work by extending the orthotic reaction force further from the axis, i.e., they lengthen the moment arm; a few mm of flange height buys real torque.

**0.6 Tissue stress model.** The practical prescription logic: identify the injured tissue, then choose mods that reduce stress on that specific tissue below its irritation threshold while keeping the foot functional. Plantar fascia → reduce arch elongation and fascial tension (support arch, heel lift slightly, fascial groove if the band itself is contact-sensitive). Posterior tibial tendon → reduce pronation moment (skive, flange, rigidity). Plantar plate/2nd MTP → reduce met head load and MTP dorsiflexion stress (met pad proximal, sometimes stiffer forefoot).

**0.7 Force–tolerance tradeoff.** Control mods (rigid shell, high flange, deep skive) generate larger corrective forces but concentrate them on smaller skin/soft-tissue areas. Comfort and compliance fail before mechanics do. The practical rule: prescribe the least aggressive combination that reduces tissue stress enough, and cushion or accommodate wherever corrective forces concentrate (hence Navicular Sweet Spot accompanying medial flanges).

**0.8 Sagittal vs frontal plane thinking.** Frontal-plane mods (posting, skives, flanges) control pronation/supination. Sagittal-plane mods (heel lifts, Morton's family, rockers, cutouts) manage forward progression and joint range. Many failed devices treat a sagittal-plane problem (blocked 1st MTP, tight calf) with frontal-plane tools. The AI should classify the presentation's dominant plane before suggesting.

## SECTION 1 — SHELL MODIFICATIONS

### 1.1 — 1st Met Cut Out
**What it is:** A relief cut in the distal medial shell edge under the 1st metatarsal head.
**Purpose:** Removes shell contact/pressure beneath the 1st met head so it can sit lower or bear less load through the device.
**Biomechanics:** Two distinct effects. (1) Pressure relief: the met head and sesamoids float over the gap, load transfers to the surrounding shell rim (0.2). (2) Sagittal-plane function: with the shell no longer propping the 1st met, the ray can plantarflex, which is a precondition for hallux dorsiflexion and windlass engagement (0.4). Peroneus longus can do its job of stabilizing the 1st ray against the ground instead of fighting the shell.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Sesamoiditis or sesamoid injury (offloading pressure under the 1st MTP)
- Plantarflexed or prominent 1st met head with focal pain or callus (IPK)
- Post-surgical or lesion offloading under the 1st met head
**Shapes offered:** Standard or **L-shape**. Both run between the same start and end points on the shell edge. Standard is a straight diagonal cut across the corner (removes a triangular section). L-shape follows an L-shaped path between those same points (removes the full corner as a squared-off section), so it takes out more shell material and creates a larger relief zone. **[LAB INPUT]** confirm the selection rule — presumably L-shape when a bigger offload area is needed (larger lesion, more prominent met head), standard for lighter relief?
**Fabrication notes:** Often paired with a soft plug or cushion topcover fill so the cutout isn't felt as an edge. **[LAB INPUT]** default cutout dimensions, whether filled with Poron/PPT by default, edge bevel practice.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Generally not combined with a Rigid Morton's Extension (opposite intents: one offloads/frees the 1st ray, the other stiffens it). Removing the medial corner slightly reduces distal-medial shell stiffness; relevant on thin or flexible shells.

### 1.2 — 1st Ray Cut Out
**What it is:** A longer relief cut along the medial shell following the entire 1st ray (met head extending proximally along the shaft).
**Purpose:** Allows the whole 1st ray to plantarflex freely instead of being held up by the shell.
**Biomechanics:** The full-length version of 1.1's sagittal effect. In functional hallux limitus, the 1st ray can't plantarflex during late midstance, so the 1st MTP can't dorsiflex, the windlass never engages, and the foot compensates with prolonged pronation and low-gear (lateral) push-off (0.4, 0.8). Cutting the shell along the whole ray removes the mechanical block at its source. Expect the center-of-pressure path to normalize toward high-gear push-off through the hallux. The tradeoff is real: the medial arch loses shell support along that strip, so net medial control drops (0.5).
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Functional hallux limitus (improves 1st MTP dorsiflexion in gait by letting the 1st ray drop)
- Plantarflexed 1st ray where full-width shell would create pressure or block motion
- Often the shell-side companion to a Kinetic Wedge or Reverse Morton's approach
**Fabrication notes:** Larger relief than a met cut out; changes shell torsional stiffness on the medial side. **[LAB INPUT]** how far proximal the cut runs by default, minimum shell width retained, rigidity implications on 3DP vs Poly.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Avoid in feet needing maximum medial control (e.g., significant PTTD): you'd be removing supinatory support from a foot that needs it. Never with Rigid Morton's Extension.

### 1.3 — 5th Met Cut Out
**What it is:** Relief cut in the distal lateral shell edge under the 5th met head.
**Purpose:** Offloads a prominent or painful 5th met head.
**Biomechanics:** Pure pressure management (0.2). Cavus and laterally-loading feet drive high pressure through the 5th met head, and unlike the 1st ray there's no windlass function to restore laterally, so this cutout is about comfort and lesion offload rather than gait re-engineering. Load moves to the adjacent rim and 4th met region — verify those tissues are healthy.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Tailor's bunion (bunionette) with plantar/lateral pressure pain
- Callus or IPK under the 5th met head
- Prominent 5th met head in cavus-type feet
**Shapes offered:** Standard (diagonal cut) or **L-shape** (squared-off cut between the same start/end points, larger relief) — same geometry as 1.1.
**Fabrication notes:** **[LAB INPUT]** default size, fill practice.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Check shoe fit; lateral edge relief plus a wide forefoot can crowd the lateral shoe wall.

### 1.4 — 5th Ray Cut Out
**What it is:** Longer lateral relief cut along the 5th ray.
**Purpose:** Allows the 5th ray to plantarflex / relieves lateral column shell pressure.
**Biomechanics:** The 5th ray is independently mobile (its own axis, like the 1st), and in rigid cavus feet it's often plantarflexed and takes early, sustained load. A shell that bridges under it concentrates pressure along the lateral column through midstance. Relieving the shell lets the ray sit down and shifts load medially toward the central mets and cuboid region. Also useful when a prominent 5th met base (styloid) rides the shell edge — that's an edge-pressure problem, not a gait problem.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Lateral column overload or pain along the 5th ray
- Plantarflexed 5th ray, rigid cavus foot with lateral pressure
- Prominent styloid process (base of 5th met) irritation at the shell edge — confirm whether the lab handles styloid relief here or as a marked accommodation **[LAB INPUT]**
**Fabrication notes:** **[LAB INPUT]** default extent, rigidity implications.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Reduces lateral shell control; caution in lateral instability presentations where the lateral shell is doing anti-supination work (0.5).

### 1.5 — Medial Flange (shell)
**What it is:** Shell extended upward along the medial arch/midfoot wall.
**Purpose:** Adds medial containment and increases pronation control beyond what arch height alone gives.
**Biomechanics:** Two mechanisms. (1) Moment arm: extending the shell up the medial wall lets the device push on the foot further from the STJ axis, generating a larger supination moment per unit of force (0.5). (2) Containment: it physically blocks the talonavicular/medial midfoot from bulging medially over the shell edge during collapse, which is exactly where flat, flexible feet escape a standard-width device. Because the corrective force now acts on the medial arch soft tissue and navicular region, pressure tolerance becomes the limiting factor (0.7).
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Adult acquired flatfoot / PTTD (esp. stage 2) needing aggressive medial control
- Severe flexible pes planus with medial midfoot collapse
- Pediatric flexible flatfoot where containment is preferred
- Often combined with medial heel skive, deeper heel cup, semi-rigid to rigid shell
**Fabrication notes:** Flange height and footwear compatibility are the practical limits. **[LAB INPUT]** default flange height, max height by shoe type, 3DP vs Poly differences.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Dress/slim footwear generally can't accept a shell flange (topcover flange may substitute). Watch navicular irritation; pair with Navicular Sweet Spot when the navicular is prominent.

### 1.6 — Lateral Flange (shell)
**What it is:** Shell extended upward along the lateral border.
**Purpose:** Resists supination/inversion; adds lateral containment.
**Biomechanics:** Mirror of 1.5 for the laterally deviated STJ axis (0.5). In cavus/sprain-prone feet, most plantar loading falls medial to a laterally positioned axis, creating chronic supination moments; the lateral flange applies pronation moment with a long lever arm and gives the cuboid/5th ray a wall to load against, and it raises the mechanical threshold for an inversion event by resisting the first degrees of heel inversion at contact. Typically works as a system with valgus posting and sometimes a lateral skive.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Chronic lateral ankle instability / recurrent inversion sprains
- Cavus foot with laterally deviated STJ axis
- Peroneal tendinopathy where reducing the inversion moment reduces tendon workload
- Often combined with valgus (lateral) posting
**Fabrication notes:** **[LAB INPUT]** default height, combination defaults with valgus posting.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Footwear fit, same as medial flange.

### 1.7 — Rigid Morton's Extension
**What it is:** Rigid shell material extended distally under the 1st MTP joint (and hallux as specified).
**Purpose:** Limits 1st MTP dorsiflexion — splints the joint through propulsion.
**Biomechanics:** This deliberately sacrifices the forefoot rocker at the 1st MTP to protect it (0.3). By stiffening the sagittal beam under the joint, dorsiflexion demand during propulsion drops, and with it the compressive/jamming forces across degenerated joint surfaces (structural hallux rigidus) or the tensile load on an injured plantar plate/sesamoid complex. The foot compensates by rolling off more laterally or through a stiffer, shorter late stance. This is the opposite philosophy to the windlass-restoration family: use it when motion itself is the pain source, not when motion is blocked functionally (0.4).
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Hallux rigidus (moderate–advanced structural limitation where motion itself is painful)
- Turf toe / plantar plate injury of the 1st MTP needing motion restriction
- Sesamoid fracture or severe sesamoiditis where 1st MTP motion aggravates
**Fabrication notes:** Rigid (shell material) vs the softer Morton's Extension in Additions — rigid restricts motion, soft cushions/supports. **[LAB INPUT]** how far distal it runs, material/thickness on 3DP vs Poly.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Never combine with Reverse Morton's, Kinetic Wedge, or 1st Ray Cut Out (opposite intents). Decision rule for the AI: painful arc / structural block → restrict (rigid extension); functional block with intact joint → facilitate (reverse Morton's / kinetic wedge / 1st ray cutout).

### 1.8 — Fascial Accommodation (as marked)
**What it is:** A groove/channel relieved into the shell along the marked course of the plantar fascia.
**Purpose:** Removes direct shell pressure from a taut or prominent fascial band or plantar fibroma.
**Biomechanics:** Arch support works partly by pushing up into the medial arch — directly along the fascia's path. In most feet that's fine; in a foot with a taut, cord-like band or a fibroma, the support itself becomes a pressure lesion generator. The channel keeps total-contact support on either side of the band while the band floats (0.2). Note this doesn't reduce fascial *tension* (that's the job of arch support geometry, heel lifts, or first-ray mods) — it only removes *contact* pressure. The AI should keep those two failure modes distinct: tension pain vs contact pain.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Prominent/cord-like medial fascial band irritated by arch contact
- Plantar fibroma or fibromatosis (marked location)
- Patients who tolerate arch support poorly due to fascial tenderness
**Fabrication notes:** Requires marked location on cast/scan or foot map. **[LAB INPUT]** default channel width/depth, whether filled with soft material.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Verify marking quality on incoming prescriptions.

### 1.9 — Navicular Sweet Spot (as marked)
**What it is:** A localized accommodation (pocket/relief) in the medial shell at the navicular.
**Purpose:** Prevents shell irritation over a prominent navicular.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Accessory navicular / os tibiale externum
- Prominent navicular tuberosity, esp. combined with high medial control (flange, skive) that increases contact
**Biomechanics:** A direct consequence of 0.7: the more supination moment you generate medially, the more force concentrates exactly where the navicular (and often tibialis posterior insertion) sits. The pocket redistributes that force to the surrounding shell so the prescription's control level stays deliverable without a compliance failure. It preserves the mechanics; it doesn't change them.
**Fabrication notes:** Marked location required. **[LAB INPUT]** default pocket size/depth.
**Cautions / conflicts:** The AI should proactively suggest it when high medial control (flange, deep skive, high arch fill) is ordered for a foot with a noted prominent navicular or accessory navicular.

### 1.10 — Heel Hole
**What it is:** An aperture cut in the heel seat of the shell, typically filled with soft material.
**Purpose:** Offloads a focal point of heel pain by removing rigid material directly beneath it.
**Biomechanics:** At heel strike, ground reaction force peaks fast (heel rocker, 0.3), and a rigid heel seat transmits that spike straight into whatever sits above it. With a spur or focal fat pad defect, the painful structure takes peak pressure every step. The aperture plus soft plug converts the focal spot into a low-stiffness zone: load flows around it into the heel cup rim, and the deep heel cup matters here because it contains and thickens the fat pad over the hole (soft tissue supplementation). Distinguish from diffuse heel pain, where global cushioning beats an aperture.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Calcaneal spur with focal point tenderness
- Fat pad atrophy with a discrete painful point
- Focal lesions (IPK, scar, post-surgical sensitivity) under the heel
- Often combined with Heel Cushion or Heel Spur/Horseshoe Pad additions
**Fabrication notes:** **[LAB INPUT]** default diameter, fill material and durometer, minimum heel cup depth required.
**Cautions / conflicts:** For diffuse (non-focal) heel pain, cushioning additions usually serve better; the AI should ask whether pain is focal before suggesting.

## SECTION 2 — ADDITIONS (top-side / topcover-level)

### 2.1 — Archfill (Soft / Firm)
**What it is:** Material filling the cavity between the shell arch and the surface layers.
**Purpose:** Soft improves comfort/tolerance under the arch; firm adds functional arch reinforcement without changing the shell.
**Biomechanics:** Firm archfill effectively raises the load-bearing stiffness of the medial arch region — more supination moment and more windlass pre-tensioning support — without touching shell geometry. Soft archfill spreads contact (0.2) and dampens the "hard edge" feel that drives early rejection of new devices (0.7). Think of it as a tolerance dial between the topcover and the shell.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Soft: patients new to orthoses, sensitive arches, comfort-focused styles
- Firm: heavier patients, flexible arches needing more support without a stiffer shell
**Fabrication notes:** **[LAB INPUT]** materials used for soft vs firm, when Archfill is included by default (Sport Comfort style lists arch fill).
**Cautions / conflicts:** —

### 2.2 — Met Pad (Full / Medium / Low; placement default 5mm past shell)
**What it is:** Dome-shaped pad placed proximal to the met heads.
**Purpose:** Redistributes forefoot load proximally off the met heads and spreads the transverse arch.
**Biomechanics:** The pad lifts the met *shafts*, which retrogradely unloads the met *heads* — load transfers from head to shaft before peak forefoot loading (0.1). Secondary effects: it splays the met heads apart (widens the interspaces, which is why it helps neuromas) and slightly reduces MTP dorsiflexion demand, easing plantar plate tension. Placement is everything: the apex must sit proximal to the heads. A pad that drifts distal sits *under* the heads and multiplies the very pressure it was meant to relieve — the single most common padding failure.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Metatarsalgia (generalized forefoot pressure pain)
- MTP capsulitis / early plantar plate strain (esp. 2nd MTP)
- Morton's neuroma (alone or with Neuroma Pad)
- Fat pad atrophy under the met heads
**Fabrication notes:** Height options Full/Medium/Low; placement default 5mm past shell edge, adjustable in mm. **[LAB INPUT]** actual heights in mm for Full/Medium/Low, material.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Start Low/Medium for first-time wearers (a full dome under a sensitive forefoot feels like a stone for the first week). Not combined with Met Bar (choose one).

### 2.3 — Met Bar (Full / Medium / Low)
**What it is:** A bar-shaped pad running across the forefoot proximal to the met heads.
**Purpose:** Same offloading principle as a met pad, distributed across multiple met heads evenly.
**Biomechanics:** Same retrograde shaft-loading mechanism as 2.2, applied across the whole parabola instead of centrally. Because it doesn't dome centrally, it produces less transverse-arch splay (less neuroma benefit) but more uniform offload, which suits globally overloaded forefeet (e.g., rheumatoid forefoot where every head is involved and fat pad has migrated distally).
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Diffuse metatarsalgia across multiple met heads
- Multiple pressure lesions/callusing across the forefoot
- Rheumatoid-type forefoot with global met head pain
**Fabrication notes:** **[LAB INPUT]** heights, coverage (which mets), material.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Decision rule: focal/central pain or neuroma → pad; diffuse multi-head pain → bar.

### 2.4 — Met Accommodation Pad (1/16" or 1/8", with cutouts, mets 1–5 selectable)
**What it is:** A forefoot padding layer with selective cutouts under the marked met head(s).
**Purpose:** Cushions the forefoot while offloading specific met heads.
**Biomechanics:** A targeted aperture strategy (0.2): the padded field raises the surrounding surface so the cut-out head floats in a well. Unlike a met pad (which works proximally via the shafts), this works *at* the heads by area redistribution, so it suits discrete lesions (IPK, ulcer risk) where you can name exactly which head must not bear load. In high-risk/diabetic feet this is the conservative default because it lowers peak pressure at a named site with minimal gait alteration.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Discrete IPK or callus under one or two met heads
- High-risk/diabetic feet needing selective offloading of a specific met head
- Focal met head pain where a dome pad alone is insufficient
**Fabrication notes:** Thickness 1/16" or 1/8"; mark which mets get cutouts per side. **[LAB INPUT]** material, when to prefer this vs a met pad.
**Cautions / conflicts:** —

### 2.5 — Morton's Extension (1/16" or 1/8", soft)
**What it is:** A padding extension under the 1st MTP/hallux at topcover level.
**Purpose:** Mildly supports/limits the 1st MTP and cushions it — a gentler alternative to the rigid shell version.
**Biomechanics:** Partially fills the space the 1st MTP would dorsiflex into and adds sagittal stiffness under the joint, trimming the end-range dorsiflexion that hurts in early hallux limitus, without fully deleting the forefoot rocker the way the rigid version does (1.7). Good titration step: soft extension → if insufficient, rigid extension.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Early/mild hallux limitus where full rigidity isn't tolerated
- Hallux rigidus patients in flexible footwear
- Trial before committing to a rigid extension
**Fabrication notes:** **[LAB INPUT]** material, guidance on 1/16 vs 1/8 selection.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Never combined with Reverse Morton's or Kinetic Wedge.

### 2.6 — Reverse Morton's Extension (1/16" or 1/8")
**What it is:** A pad under met heads 2–5 leaving the 1st met head free (dropped).
**Purpose:** Encourages 1st ray plantarflexion; offloads the 1st met head/sesamoids.
**Biomechanics:** Raises the floor under mets 2–5 so the 1st met head sits relatively lower. Two payoffs: (1) windlass facilitation — the plantarflexed 1st ray permits hallux dorsiflexion (0.4); (2) load shift — the sesamoid complex gives up load to the elevated lesser mets, which is why it's a first-line sesamoiditis mod. It's the addition-level sibling of the 1st Ray Cut Out; use both together for a strong facilitation prescription.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Functional hallux limitus
- Sesamoiditis (offloads the 1st met head by raising 2–5)
- Complements 1st Ray Cut Out / Kinetic Wedge approach
**Fabrication notes:** **[LAB INPUT]** thickness selection guidance, material.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Never with Morton's Extension (either type). Confirm lesser met heads are healthy — you're sending load to them (0.1).

### 2.7 — Heel Cushion (1/16" or 1/8")
**What it is:** Soft cushioning layer in the heel seat.
**Purpose:** Shock absorption and diffuse heel pressure relief.
**Biomechanics:** Works at the heel rocker (0.3) by extending the time over which heel-strike force is applied — lower peak force, same impulse — and by spreading contact across the whole heel seat (0.2). This is the diffuse-pain counterpart to the focal tools (Heel Hole, Horseshoe Pad). It also partially substitutes for a thinned fat pad, which is fundamentally a lost-cushioning problem.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Fat pad atrophy (diffuse)
- General heel pain in heavy heel-strikers, hard-surface occupations
- Older patients, arthritic heel pain
- Companion to Heel Hole or Heel Spur Pad for focal problems
**Fabrication notes:** **[LAB INPUT]** material and durometer per thickness.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Adds heel stack height — check heel cup depth/shoe fit; a deeper heel cup may be needed to keep the heel contained.

### 2.8 — Heel Spur / Horseshoe Pad
**What it is:** A U-shaped (horseshoe) pad around the central heel, relieved at the tender point.
**Purpose:** Builds up around a focal painful spot so the spot itself floats.
**Biomechanics:** The additive version of the Heel Hole: instead of cutting a well into the shell, you raise the land around the lesion. Same pressure logic (0.2), softer implementation, reversible, and stackable with a heel cushion. The open end of the U typically faces distally so the fascia's insertion path isn't compressed. Choose hole vs horseshoe (or both) based on severity and how much stack height the shoe allows.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Calcaneal spur / insertional plantar fasciitis with focal tenderness
- Focal fat pad lesions where a full cushion still leaves point pressure
**Fabrication notes:** **[LAB INPUT]** material, thickness, default orientation of the opening.
**Cautions / conflicts:** The AI should clarify pain distribution (focal vs diffuse) before suggesting.

### 2.9 — Kinetic Wedge
**What it is:** A forefoot addition that supports mets 2–5 while allowing the 1st ray to plantarflex through propulsion.
**Purpose:** Restores 1st MTP dorsiflexion mechanics in gait (Dananberg's sagittal-plane facilitation approach to functional hallux limitus).
**Biomechanics:** Functionally in the same family as the Reverse Morton's, but conceived dynamically: it's about what happens through propulsion, not just static met head height. By giving the 1st ray somewhere to go while the lesser mets stay supported, the windlass engages, CoP tracks medially into high-gear push-off, and the proximal compensations (late-stance pronation, reduced hip extension) unwind (0.4, 0.8). Dananberg's work links restored sagittal-plane flow to relief of postural symptoms up the chain — worth remembering when the presentation includes "foot fine, but knee/hip/low back complaints with gait findings."
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Functional hallux limitus
- Gait findings of blocked 1st MTP dorsiflexion with compensations (low-gear push-off, hallux dorsiflexion visible in swing but blocked in stance)
**Fabrication notes:** **[LAB INPUT]** exact construction as Leo Lab builds it, material, and how it practically differs from Reverse Morton's on your menu — the AI needs your house decision rule between them.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Never with Morton's Extension (either type).

### 2.10 — Neuroma Pad (between mets __ and __)
**What it is:** A small teardrop/dome pad placed between two specified met heads.
**Purpose:** Spreads the adjacent met heads to decompress the interdigital nerve.
**Biomechanics:** The interdigital nerve gets compressed between met heads and sheared under the deep transverse metatarsal ligament during forefoot loading and toe-off. A pad placed *between* the heads (not under them) lifts and splays the adjacent shafts, opening the interspace and reducing both compression and shear at exactly the phase it hurts. Combined with a met pad, you get global proximal offload plus targeted interspace decompression.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Morton's neuroma — most commonly the 3rd interspace (between mets 3 and 4), sometimes the 2nd (between 2 and 3)
- Interdigital irritation/bursitis
**Fabrication notes:** Prescription specifies the interspace. **[LAB INPUT]** size, material, default height.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Placement precision matters; the AI should always confirm which interspace.

### 2.11 — Medial Flange (topcover)
**What it is:** Flange effect built at topcover level rather than shell.
**Purpose:** Milder medial containment/comfort where a shell flange is too aggressive or won't fit the footwear.
**Biomechanics:** Same moment-arm principle as 1.5 but with compliant material, so it delivers containment and proprioceptive edge feedback more than hard corrective torque. Appropriate when the goal is "keep the midfoot on the device" rather than "fight a big pronation moment."
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Moderate pronation control needs in dress or low-volume footwear
- Patients intolerant of rigid shell flanges
**Fabrication notes:** **[LAB INPUT]** construction, height limits.
**Cautions / conflicts:** —

### 2.12 — Lateral Flange (topcover)
**What it is:** Topcover-level lateral flange.
**Purpose:** Milder lateral containment; comfort-level inversion resistance.
**Biomechanics:** As 2.11, mirrored: containment and early-inversion feedback without rigid lateral torque. A reasonable step-down from a shell lateral flange for mild instability or tight footwear.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Mild lateral instability in footwear that can't take a shell flange
**Fabrication notes:** **[LAB INPUT]** construction, height limits.
**Cautions / conflicts:** —

### 2.13 — Arch Cookie / D-Pad (1/16" or 1/8")
**What it is:** A supplementary medial arch pad (cookie/D-shaped).
**Purpose:** Boosts arch support locally without a new shell.
**Biomechanics:** A local stiffness/height bump in the medial arch: more contact area under the sustentaculum/navicular region, slightly more supination support, more fascial pre-tension support in midstance. It's a fine-tuning tool, not a control tool — think of it as the smallest increment on the same dial as firm archfill → shell change.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Patient wants more arch "feel" or support on an existing-style device
- Mild medial support augmentation where a shell change isn't warranted
**Fabrication notes:** **[LAB INPUT]** material, placement rules.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Conflicts with the intent of a Fascial Accommodation (one adds arch contact, the other removes it) — flag if both appear on one prescription.

## SECTION 3 — RELATED PRESCRIPTION FIELDS (phase 2, brief)
These interact with the modifications above and the AI needs them for complete recommendations:
- **Heel Skive (Med/Lat, mm):** intrinsic wedging of the heel seat. A medial skive shifts the heel cup's reaction force medially under the calcaneus → supination moment at the STJ (Kirby); pairs with PTTD/pronation prescriptions. Lateral skive mirrors it for cavus/supination control.
- **Heel Lift (mm):** raises the calcaneus → reduces Achilles/gastroc-soleus tension and ankle dorsiflexion demand; used for LLD, insertional Achilles pathology, equinus accommodation. Note it also slightly plantarflexes the foot, shifting load forefoot-ward (0.1) — relevant if metatarsalgia coexists.
- **Posting (rearfoot/forefoot, intrinsic/extrinsic, varus/valgus, degrees):** the core frontal-plane torque tools (0.5); interact with flanges and skives — the AI should treat skive + flange + post as one additive "medial control budget," not three independent items.
- **Heel Cup Depth (9–20mm+):** deeper cups increase calcaneal grip (frontal-plane control) and fat pad containment (cushioning); constrained by footwear depth.
- **Orthotic Rigidity (Poly vs 3DP, Flexible→Rigid):** stiffness sets how much corrective force the geometry actually delivers vs deflects away; 3DP (MJF) rigidity behavior and lattice/thickness equivalences **[LAB INPUT]**.
- **Extra Cushioning (Full length vs Forefoot only; Puff/Poron; 1/16"–1/8"):** full length for global cushioning needs (cavus, fat pad loss, hard-surface occupations); forefoot-only when the need is metatarsal-region cushioning without adding heel stack height. Remember every layer costs shoe volume.
- **Cast Dressing (Tight to Arch / Moderate (default) / Min −3mm / Max +3mm):** this is the arch-congruency dial. Tight-to-arch fills the cast closer to the true arch contour → more aggressive, more support, less forgiveness; minimum dressing behaves similarly by removing plaster allowance. Maximum dressing (+3mm) lowers effective arch contact → gentler, more tolerant, less control. Practical use: tight for control prescriptions in tolerant feet (0.7 caution applies), maximum for sensitive arches, first-time wearers, fascial tenderness. This interacts directly with archfill and arch cookie choices — treat arch aggressiveness as one dial across all three.
- **Orthotic Width (Regular / Wide / Narrow −mm):** wide for broad feet, control builds needing full shell contact, and heavier patients; narrow (specify mm) to fit low-volume footwear — accept that narrowing trims the shell's lever arms and containment slightly. If a prescription pairs "narrow" with high control demands, flag the tension.
- **Topcover Length (Full / Sulcus / 3/4):** full length for most builds and whenever forefoot additions (met pads, extensions, cushioning) need a carrier over them; sulcus (ends just behind the toes) keeps toe box volume while still carrying forefoot additions; 3/4 (ends behind the met heads) maximizes shoe versatility and forefoot room but CANNOT carry met-head-level or toe-level additions — flag any prescription combining 3/4 length with forefoot extensions/pads that need distal coverage.
- **Bottom Cover (Vinyl / J-Suede / Cordura / Puff / Nyplex):** interface between device and shoe bed — durability, grip (device migration in the shoe), and slight cushioning differences. **[LAB INPUT]** when the lab steers each.
- **Skid Plate:** **[LAB INPUT]** exactly what the lab's skid plate is and when it's ordered (wear protection at high-abrasion zones? specific sports?). The AI should not guess on this one until defined.

## SECTION 4 — HOW TO THINK THROUGH A PRESCRIPTION (the tech's framework)

The pedorthist has already assessed and identified the problem. This framework organizes how to translate that finding into a device. The AI should walk this order with the user when a full prescription is being built (vs a single-mod question).

**Step 0 — THE MASTER FORK: distribute pressure, or modify movement?**

Before anything else, the AI classifies the goal. Every mod on the form serves one of two masters, and the first question that sorts any request is:

> **"Is the problem WHERE the load lands, or HOW the foot moves?"**

**BRANCH A — DISTRIBUTE PRESSURE** (the tissue hurts under load; motion itself is fine)
The complaint is pain at a contact site: under a met head, at a spur point, over the navicular, along the fascial band, under a thinned fat pad. The foot's mechanics may be normal or abnormal, but the *ask* is to change where force lands.
Then branch again on focality:
- **A1 — Focal** (one nameable point) → *make the point float*: apertures and reliefs. Heel Hole, 1st/5th Met Cut Out (standard or L by size), Met Accommodation Pad with cutouts, Fascial Accommodation, Navicular Sweet Spot, Heel Spur/Horseshoe Pad.
- **A2 — Regional** (one zone, e.g., all met heads, whole heel) → *shift load to a neighbor that can take it*: Met Pad, Met Bar (shift heads→shafts), deeper heel cup (spread over more heel), reverse Morton's used purely as a sesamoid offloader.
- **A3 — Diffuse/global** (everything hurts, poor native cushioning) → *lower peak force everywhere*: Heel Cushion, Extra Cushioning, cushioned/diabetic topcovers, soft archfill, accommodative philosophy overall.
Sanity check for Branch A: name where the load is going (0.1) and confirm that tissue is healthy.

**BRANCH B — MODIFY MOVEMENT** (the way the foot moves is producing the problem)
The complaint traces to motion or position: collapsing arch, inverting ankle, a 1st MTP that hurts through range or won't move, excess pronation loading a tendon. The *ask* is to change torque, range, or timing.
Then branch on what's wrong with the motion:
- **B1 — Too much motion / wrong position** (control it) → skives, posting, flanges (shell), shell rigidity, heel cup depth, firm archfill. Direction decides side: pronation problems → medial stack; supination/instability → lateral stack. Treat the stack as one budget (0.5, Step 3).
- **B2 — Motion itself is painful** (restrict it) → Rigid Morton's Extension (soft Morton's as the step-down). Deliberately sacrifice the rocker to protect the joint.
- **B3 — Motion is blocked but the joint is healthy** (restore it) → 1st Ray Cut Out, Reverse Morton's, Kinetic Wedge. Remove what's propping the 1st ray so the windlass can work (0.4).
B2 and B3 are mutually exclusive philosophies at the same joint — the critical fork from 6.1 question 7.

**Dual-role mods (the AI should state which role it's using):**
- *1st Met Cut Out*: Branch A1 (sesamoid/met head offload) AND Branch B3 (frees the ray). Often both at once — say so.
- *Reverse Morton's*: A2 offloader for sesamoids, B3 facilitator for functional hallux limitus.
- *Deep heel cup*: A2 pressure spreader (fat pad containment) AND B1 control (calcaneal grip).
- *Heel Lift*: primarily B (reduces Achilles/ankle demand) but with an A side-effect — it shifts load forefoot-ward; check 5.7.
- *Flanges (topcover)*: nominally B1 but mostly containment/comfort — treat as the mild end of the control budget.

**Mixed presentations run both branches.** A PTTD foot with a prominent navicular is B1 (control the collapse) + A1 (sweet spot where the control force lands). Metatarsalgia in an over-pronating foot is A2 at the forefoot + B1 at the rearfoot. The AI should explicitly label which mods in its answer serve which branch — that's what makes a recommendation read as reasoning instead of a parts list.

**Step 1 — Name the tissue and the failure mode.**
What structure is overloaded, and is the problem *tension* (fascia, tendons), *compression* (met heads, spur point, neuroma), *motion* (painful joint range), or *position/instability* (collapsing arch, inverting heel)? Each failure mode has its own tool family:
- Tension → support the arch, reduce elongation demand, heel lift where the Achilles chain is involved
- Compression → redistribute (pads), accommodate (apertures, cutouts, sweet spots), cushion
- Motion → restrict it (rigid Morton's) or restore it (reverse Morton's, kinetic wedge, 1st ray cutout) — pick a side, never both
- Position/instability → control (skive, posting, flange, rigidity, heel cup depth), treated as one combined budget

**Step 2 — Decide the device philosophy: functional, accommodative, or hybrid.**
- Functional: semi-rigid to rigid shell, control features, minimal padding. For mechanical problems in feet that can tolerate correction.
- Accommodative: flexible shell or EVA, total contact, cushioning, apertures. For rigid deformities, fragile tissue, high-risk feet (diabetic, rheumatoid), fat pad loss.
- Hybrid (most real prescriptions): a functional base with targeted accommodations. The Leo Lab form is built for this — shell + rigidity choices set the base, additions layer the accommodation.
Rule of thumb: correct the flexible, accommodate the rigid. If a deformity doesn't reduce on exam, don't fight it with the device — make room for it.

**Step 3 — Set the control budget (rearfoot first, then midfoot, then forefoot).**
For pronation-related prescriptions, control comes from stacking: shell rigidity → heel cup depth → medial skive → rearfoot varus post → medial flange → firm archfill. Start with the mild stack and escalate on known-difficult presentations (heavier patients, PTTD, failed previous devices). For supination/instability, mirror it laterally: valgus post, lateral skive, lateral flange, flat/wide heel cup.
Patient factors that raise the budget: body weight, activity level (running multiplies forces), flexible/collapsing foot, ligamentous laxity. Factors that lower it: age, sensitive soft tissue, first-time wearer, dress footwear, rigid foot type.

**Step 4 — Layer the offloading/comfort mods for the named tissue.**
This is where Sections 1–2 plug in. One primary offloading strategy per painful site; add a companion mod only when it works through a *different* mechanism (e.g., Heel Hole [aperture] + Heel Cushion [damping] is a valid pair; Met Pad + Met Bar is not).

**Step 5 — Reality-check against the shoe and the patient.**
Every mod has a footwear cost (stack height, flange height, width, heel cup depth) and a tolerance cost (0.7). Check: does the intended shoe physically accept this build? Will a first-time wearer tolerate it in week one? If not, either step down the aggression or note a break-in plan. **[LAB INPUT]** the lab's footwear compatibility rules per style (Dress/Slim vs Sport vs Diabetic) — which mods you routinely reject or substitute per style.

**Step 6 — Scan for conflicts.**
Run the conflict list (Section 6.2) before finalizing. If two mods pull opposite directions, the prescription is telling you the problem hasn't been fully named yet — go back to Step 1.

## SECTION 5 — PRESENTATION → PRESCRIPTION QUICK REFERENCE

Common packages for clinician-identified presentations. These are starting points to adapt, not defaults to copy blindly. The clinician owns the final call. **[LAB INPUT]** — Pratik to confirm/adjust each package against what Leo Lab actually sees prescribed and what works.

**5.1 Plantar heel pain — fascial origin (clinician-identified plantar fasciitis)**
- Base: semi-rigid shell, moderate–deep heel cup (14–16mm+), good arch congruency (tight-to-arch cast dressing if tolerated)
- Tension control: firm archfill or arch cookie if more medial support is needed; small heel lift when calf tightness is part of the picture
- Contact relief: fascial accommodation if the band itself is tender to arch contact
- Insertional/focal point: heel spur/horseshoe pad; heel hole if sharply focal
- Comfort: heel cushion 1/16–1/8"
- Escalation path: arch support level → heel lift → focal offload → rigidity

**5.2 Plantar heel pain — fat pad atrophy (diffuse, "walking on bone")**
- Base: deeper heel cup (contains and centralizes remaining fat pad), semi-flexible to semi-rigid shell
- Cushioning: heel cushion 1/8", cushioned topcover (Poron/PPT family), extra cushioning full length for global loss
- Focal defect: heel hole with soft plug
- Avoid: aggressive rigid heel seat with no interface

**5.3 Medial arch collapse / posterior tibial overload (clinician-identified PTTD, stage-appropriate)**
- Base: semi-rigid → rigid shell, deep heel cup (16mm+), tight-to-arch
- Control stack: medial heel skive (2–4mm typical range **[LAB INPUT]** house defaults), rearfoot varus post, medial flange (shell)
- Protect the pressure points: navicular sweet spot when the navicular/accessory navicular is prominent
- Tolerance: soft archfill or cushioned topcover over the firm base if the arch is sensitive
- Footwear: confirm depth/width before committing to flange height

**5.4 First MTP pain — motion is the problem (clinician-identified hallux rigidus / advanced limitus)**
- Restrict: rigid Morton's extension (or soft Morton's 1/8" as trial/step-down)
- Base: rigid forefoot behavior matters more than rearfoot control here
- Never add: reverse Morton's, kinetic wedge, 1st ray cutout

**5.5 First MTP pain — motion is blocked but joint intact (clinician-identified functional hallux limitus)**
- Facilitate: 1st ray cutout (shell) + reverse Morton's or kinetic wedge (addition) **[LAB INPUT]** house rule between RM and KW
- Base: avoid overly stiff medial forefoot; standard arch support otherwise per foot type
- Never add: Morton's extension (either type)

**5.6 Sesamoid pain (clinician-identified sesamoiditis/fracture)**
- Offload: 1st met cut out (standard or L-shape by lesion size) with soft fill, and/or reverse Morton's
- If motion aggravates (fracture, severe): rigid Morton's extension instead — restrict, don't facilitate; decide one philosophy
- Cushion: forefoot extra cushioning 1/16–1/8"

**5.7 Central metatarsalgia / plantar plate strain (clinician-identified)**
- Offload: met pad (medium default; low for first-timers; full for heavy/established wearers) placed proximal — default 5mm past shell, adjust per fit feedback
- Focal single head: met accommodation pad with cutout under the named head
- Diffuse all-heads (incl. rheumatoid-type): met bar instead of pad
- Cushion: forefoot puff/Poron 1/16–1/8"; consider fat pad status
- Check: heel lift elsewhere in the Rx increases forefoot load — rebalance if both present

**5.8 Interdigital nerve pain (clinician-identified Morton's neuroma)**
- Decompress: neuroma pad in the named interspace (confirm which — commonly 3rd)
- Offload: met pad (medium) alongside
- Footwear note: forefoot room matters as much as the device; flag narrow toe boxes

**5.9 Lateral instability / recurrent inversion (clinician-identified)**
- Control stack: valgus rearfoot posting, lateral heel skive, lateral flange (shell), flat wide heel seat, moderate heel cup
- Avoid: high medial arch fill or varus posting artifacts that tip the heel laterally
- Cavus + lateral overload: add 5th met/ray relief as needed, cushioned topcover (cavus feet are poor shock absorbers)

**5.10 Cavus foot, global overload (rigid high arch, heel + forefoot loading)**
- Philosophy: accommodate the rigidity — total contact, semi-flexible shell, maximum load spreading
- Cushioning: full-length extra cushioning, cushioned topcover
- Focal relief: met head cutouts/accommodation pads at pressure sites, heel cushion
- Control only as needed: lateral flange/valgus post if instability coexists

**5.11 High-risk foot (clinician-identified diabetic/neuropathic, protective prescription)**
- Base: accommodative philosophy, Plastazote-type total-contact interface
- Offload named sites: met accommodation pad with cutouts, heel hole with soft plug where indicated
- Avoid: aggressive corrective features that concentrate force on insensate skin
- Everything soft-edged: bevels, no abrupt pad margins **[LAB INPUT]** house finishing standards for diabetic builds

**5.12 Leg length discrepancy (clinician-measured)**
- Heel lift on the short side (mm per clinician); note forefoot load shift (5.7 check)
- Larger corrections belong at the shoe (external buildup) — flag when the requested lift exceeds what the device/shoe can take **[LAB INPUT]** max in-device lift by style

**5.13 Achilles / posterior heel pain (clinician-identified tendinopathy)**
- The location fork matters: *insertional* (at the heel bone) vs *mid-portion* (above it) — clinician specifies
- Both: heel lift bilateral (lifting only one side creates an artificial LLD) — reduces gastroc-soleus/Achilles tension and ankle dorsiflexion demand (0.3, heel/ankle rocker) **[LAB INPUT]** default lift mm range
- Insertional: also check heel counter contact — the problem is often shoe-structure compression at the insertion; a deep heel cup can help center the heel away from the counter, cushioned topcover at the heel
- If over-pronation is loading the medial Achilles fibers (whipping): add the medial control stack per 5.3, moderate level
- Watch: heel lift shifts load forefoot-ward — rebalance if forefoot complaints coexist (5.7 check)

**5.14 Pediatric heel pain (clinician-identified Sever's / calcaneal apophysitis)**
- Simple builds win: heel lift (small, bilateral) + heel cushion 1/8" — reduces Achilles traction on the apophysis and dampens heel-strike load
- Base: semi-flexible to semi-rigid per weight/activity; deep enough heel cup to keep the cushion under the heel
- If the child over-pronates significantly (clinician finding): moderate medial control; avoid over-building — this condition is self-limiting and tolerance rules (0.7)
- Growth note: flag expected outgrow/replacement interval **[LAB INPUT]** the lab's pediatric sizing/replacement guidance

**5.15 Hallux valgus / bunion (clinician-identified)**
- Set expectations: an in-shoe device doesn't straighten the toe; the orthotic job is (a) slow the mechanical driver and (b) offload the painful spots
- Driver control: many HAV feet hypermobile/over-pronate through the 1st ray — medial control stack (moderate), firm arch support to reduce 1st-ray dorsal drift loading
- Offload: 1st met head pressure → 1st Met Cut Out or Met Accommodation Pad with 1st cutout; transfer lesions under 2–3 (common as the 1st offloads itself) → met pad
- Late/rigid HAV with joint pain through range: treat as 5.4 logic (restrict) — soft or rigid Morton's extension per severity
- Footwear reality: forefoot width does more for bunion comfort than any pad; flag narrow toe boxes

**5.16 Proximal symptoms with gait findings (clinician-identified MTSS/shin splints, patellofemoral, hip/low-back with gait link)**
- The AI's role here is strictly translation: the clinician has already linked the proximal symptom to a foot mechanism; the build targets that mechanism
- Excess pronation identified as the driver (common in MTSS, medial knee stress): medial control stack per 5.3, dosed to weight/activity; running loads may justify one step more control than walking-only
- Blocked sagittal flow identified (functional hallux limitus with proximal compensation — the Dananberg pattern, 2.9): facilitation family per 5.5
- Shock-dominant presentations (hard-surface running, cavus): cushioning layer + semi-flexible base
- If no foot-level mechanism has been named: that's a clinical determination — redirect per 6.4 rather than guessing a build

## SECTION 6 — SANITY CHECKS, CONFLICTS, AND TROUBLESHOOTING

**6.0 The stakes: remakes are full cost.** There is no free remake. Every recommendation should be framed to survive first dispense: prefer adjustable/step-down options when uncertainty is high (soft before rigid Morton's, medium before full met pad, moderate before maximum control), and say explicitly when a cheaper path exists — sometimes the right answer is a chairside adjustment, a break-in period, or no new modification at all, not a new device. Overprescribing is a failure mode the AI should actively avoid.

**6.1 Questions the AI should ask before recommending (when missing):**
1. Is the pain focal or diffuse? (drives aperture vs cushion decisions)
2. Which tissue/structure has the clinician identified? (drives the tool family)
3. Foot type and flexibility — does the deformity reduce? (correct vs accommodate)
4. Footwear the device must fit (style limits the menu)
5. Patient weight/activity (sets the control budget)
6. First device or replacement? If replacement — what failed on the last one? (the single most informative question)
7. For 1st MTP presentations: is motion painful, or blocked? (restrict vs facilitate — the critical fork)

**6.2 Conflict list (flag if these appear together):**
- Morton's Extension (rigid or soft) + Reverse Morton's / Kinetic Wedge / 1st Ray Cutout — opposite philosophies
- Met Pad + Met Bar — pick one
- Arch Cookie / firm archfill + Fascial Accommodation — one adds arch contact, the other removes it; possible together only if the cookie sits clear of the marked band, flag for confirmation
- Medial flange + dress/slim style — physically incompatible; offer topcover flange
- High medial control stack (skive+post+flange) + prominent navicular with no sweet spot — predictable irritation; suggest adding it
- Heavy heel lift + existing metatarsalgia mods — lift shifts load forward; rebalance forefoot offload
- 1st/5th ray cutouts on a foot that needs maximum respective-side control — control leak
- Deep heel cup + heel cushion stack in a shallow shoe — heel will ride out of the shoe; check depth

**6.3 Troubleshooting a device that isn't working (common failure → first suspects):**
- "Arch is killing me" → too much arch too fast (step down/soft archfill, break-in), fascial band contact (fascial accommodation), or cast dressing too tight to arch
- "Met pad makes it worse" → pad drifted or placed too distal, sitting under the heads; re-place proximally, or the presentation wanted an accommodation pad, not a dome
- "Heel pain unchanged" → treated diffuse pain with focal tools or vice versa; recheck focality; check heel cup depth and whether the heel sits centered
- "Feels like I'm falling outward/rolling out" → over-corrected medially (too much skive/post/flange); reduce the stack
- "Navicular/ankle bone rubbing" → flange or arch height contacting prominence; sweet spot or lower flange
- "Device fine, shoe doesn't fit anymore" → stack height/flange exceeded shoe volume; slimmer topcover, lower heel cup, or style change
- "Squeaking/edge feel at cutouts" → unfilled or unbeveled cutout edges **[LAB INPUT]** standard fill/bevel practice
- "Worked for months, now sore again" → topcover/padding compressed (materials bottom out); refurbish interval **[LAB INPUT]** expected service life of pads/covers by material

**6.4 Out-of-scope triggers (AI must redirect, not answer):**
- Any request to identify/diagnose the condition from symptoms ("what does this patient have?")
- Requests to interpret imaging, labs, or neurological findings
- Red-flag descriptions (open wounds on insensate feet, suspected fracture, infection signs, acute trauma) → advise clinical management first; a device Rx can wait
- Medication, injection, or surgical questions
Standard response shape: "That's a clinical determination for you as the treating clinician — once you've identified the target tissue/goal, I can help translate it into the device build."

## SECTION 7 — DEVICE-LEVEL PRESCRIPTIONS (styles with their own logic)

### 7.1 — UCBL
**What it is:** A deep-seated device with high medial AND lateral walls plus a deep heel cup — the shell wraps the whole rearfoot/midfoot (University of California Biomechanics Laboratory design). On the form: Poly Pro shell, Black Microcell 1/8" topcover.
**Purpose:** Maximum containment. Where a medial flange adds one wall, the UCBL builds the whole box: it grips the calcaneus and blocks both medial collapse and forefoot abduction (the "too many toes" escape route that standard devices can't hold).
**Biomechanics:** Control by total containment rather than by wedging alone. The high trimlines let the device apply corrective force around the whole perimeter of the rearfoot complex — calcaneal eversion, talonavicular sag, and forefoot abduction are all resisted at once (0.5 taken to its limit). This is the ceiling of the in-shoe control budget: the step beyond flange + skive + post, and the step before bracing (SMO/AFO territory, out of scope).
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Severe flexible pediatric flatfoot (classic use case)
- Severe adult flexible collapse where flange-level control failed or won't hold
- Presentations with significant forefoot abduction on top of medial collapse
**Fabrication notes:** Footwear is the hard constraint — high trimlines need depth and a stable heel counter. **[LAB INPUT]** trimline height defaults, age/weight guidance, casting requirements (degree of correction held in cast), shoe requirements the lab specifies.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Rigid deformity is a contraindication in principle (correct the flexible, accommodate the rigid — Step 2). Skin tolerance at trimlines needs review at dispense. Not a dress-shoe device, ever.
**Historical note / synonyms:** Clinicians may reference Whitman or Roberts plates (the historical deep-seated steel/leather designs) or say "Whitman-style" — in modern practice these requests resolve to the UCBL conversation. The Whitman plate used high medial/lateral walls with a similar containment philosophy; UCBL is its modern descendant.

### 7.2 — Gait Plate (Induce In-toeing / Induce Out-toeing)
**What it is:** A full-length or sulcus-length rigid plate with the distal edge cut on a specific diagonal, in two versions on the form: induce in-toeing or induce out-toeing. Design-your-own Poly Pro shell base.
**Purpose:** Nudges the transverse-plane line of progression — used almost exclusively in children whose rotational alignment is expected to be flexible.
**Biomechanics:** The angled distal edge changes where the foot can break (flex) at toe-off. To induce OUT-toeing (the common version, for in-toeing kids): the plate extends further distally on the medial side, so the medial forefoot can't flex early; the easiest roll-off path becomes lateral→external, and the swing limb follows into more out-toe. To induce IN-toeing (for out-toeing kids, less common): the geometry mirrors — the plate runs long laterally, blocking early lateral break so the roll-off path steers medial→internal. Either way it's a timing-and-path trick at the forefoot rocker (0.3), not a torque device — it guides, it doesn't twist.
**Typical clinical triggers:**
- Pediatric in-toeing (clinician-identified, flexible, non-neurological) → out-toe-inducing plate
- Pediatric out-toeing → in-toe-inducing plate
**Fabrication notes:** **[LAB INPUT]** age range the lab accepts, length (full vs sulcus) practice, edge geometry defaults, expected wear duration guidance the lab gives clinics.
**Cautions / conflicts:** Rigid or structural rotational deformity (femoral/tibial torsion that doesn't present as flexible) is a clinical determination — the AI should note gait plates address flexible presentations and defer suitability to the clinician. Combining with aggressive frontal-plane control changes the roll-off path; flag combined requests for clinician confirmation.
